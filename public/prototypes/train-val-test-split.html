Phase 3 · The Three Splits
Train, Val, and Test each have one job
They're not interchangeable. Using the wrong split for the wrong job is how projects go wrong.
🔄 Iterate🔒
Training — fit weights on train setVal — compare & pickTest
The test split is locked during the entire development process. You only open it once, at the very end, to get an honest final number.
Train
Learn parameters
Runs gradient descent. Updates every weight in the network.
  • More data usually helps
  • Train distribution matters less — it can include messier sources
  • Never used for model selection
Val / Dev
Choose ideas
Compare architectures, hyperparameters, and data strategies. Pick the winner.
  • Must reflect future users — this is your compass
  • If it's wrong, every decision is wrong
  • Can be looked at many times
Test
Final honest check
Gives the one number you can trust. Only valid if you haven't touched it during development.
  • Must also reflect future users
  • Opened exactly once
  • Every extra peek makes this number less trustworthy
Phase 4 · The Right Method
Hold-out works. Until your data doesn't cooperate.
The three splits tell you what each piece of data is for. This tells you how to actually cut it. The right method depends on how much data you have — and what kind.
What's your situation?
How much training data do you have?
Does the order of your data matter?
Can the same source appear multiple times in your data?
e.g. multiple records from the same user, patient, store, or device
Is this an imbalanced classification problem?
e.g. one class is ≤10% of the data — fraud detection, rare disease, defect detection
Simple
Hold-out Split
Split once. Train on one part, evaluate on another. Simple, fast, and the right choice when you have plenty of data.
Train 70%
Val 15%
Test 15%
Proportions vary by project. 70/15/15 is a common starting point.
⚠️ Unstable with small datasets. If your sample is small, the specific examples that land in val can swing your score by 10–15 points. The number you see may not be representative of real performance.
Robust
K-Fold Cross-Validation
Divide data into k equal folds. Train k times, each time holding out a different fold as val. Average the k scores.
Fold 1: 82%
Fold 2: 79%
Fold 3: 84%
Fold 4: 81%
Fold 5: 83%
Average: 81.8%
⚠️ K-Fold replaces val — not test. Many people train with K-Fold, take the average as their "final score", and skip the test set entirely. That's wrong. The average is for model selection. Test is still opened once, at the very end, on data the whole training loop never touched.

⚠️ If your classes are roughly balanced, regular K-Fold is fine. Stratified K-Fold is for imbalanced problems — fraud detection, rare disease classification, or any case where one class is significantly underrepresented.
Grouped Data
Group K-Fold
Same as K-Fold, but the split ensures that all rows from the same source (user, patient, device) land in the same fold. Prevents the model from "recognizing" a source it saw during training.
Regular K-Fold
F1ABAC
F2BCAB
Patient A appears in both folds — data leakage
Group K-Fold
F1AAA
F2BBCC
Each patient in exactly one fold ✓
⚠️ You must explicitly pass group labels to the splitter. If you forget, sklearn will silently fall back to regular K-Fold and your val scores will be optimistically inflated.
Time Series
Walk-forward Validation
Time-series data cannot be shuffled. Training on "future" data to predict "past" data gives you a score that looks great but means nothing. The train window grows forward; val always comes after train.

⚠️ The most silent mistake in ML projects. A single df.sample() or shuffle=True before your split will leak future data into training. The model will look like it works. It won't.
One rule that applies to every method above
K-Fold, Stratified K-Fold, Group K-Fold — all of these replace val. None of them replace test.

No matter which method you use for model selection, test is still reserved, still locked, and still opened exactly once at the end.

Phase 6 · The Second Trap
Your dev set is perfect. But you kept peeking at test.
There are two ways to get a misleading final number. Phase 5 showed you the first. Here's the second.
The setup
The team fixed their splits. Dev and test both reflect real users. They're doing everything right — except one thing: every time they feel uncertain, they check the test score "just to see".
What they're doing
  • ✅ Dev set matches real users
  • ✅ Test set matches real users
  • ✅ Train on train, evaluate on dev
  • ❌ Test opened multiple times during development

Test has never been touched. When you open it at the end, the number will be a genuine surprise — and that surprise is the point.
Two rules. That's it.
1
Dev and test must reflect the users you actually care about.
Not the data that was easy to collect.
2
Test is opened once. At the very end.
Every additional peek makes the number less honest.
In practice, this looks like:
# After collecting your data train_data = data[data['source'] == 'train'] val_data = data[data['source'] == 'val'] ← matches real users test_data = data[data['source'] == 'test'] ← locked until the end # During development: only evaluate on val model.evaluate(val_data) ← use this to compare models # At the very end, once: model.evaluate(test_data) ← final honest number
